Overview

Payout Engine handles the merchant-to-beneficiary transfer lifecycle across three independent bank rails — IMPS, NEFT, and RTGS — each with its own limits and fee rules. The highest-financial-risk boundary in the whole module is retry idempotency: a network blip during a bank submission must never cause the platform to resubmit a transfer that already succeeded on the bank's side.

In plain terms, Payout is the side of the platform that sends money out — it lets a merchant pay a beneficiary (a vendor, employee, or partner) through whichever bank rail fits the amount and urgency, tracks the transfer to a definitive outcome, and reconciles it against the merchant's ledger. Underneath the UI, the module is composed of roughly 38 cooperating microservices:

How It Works & My Role

Core Payout Flow

  1. Merchant adds a Beneficiary for payout
  2. Beneficiary passes verification and approval gates
  3. Merchant initiates a payout, choosing IMPS, NEFT, or RTGS
  4. The Commercial & GST Engine calculates the applicable fee
  5. The Retry Service guarantees an idempotent bank submission
  6. The Ledger updates and Reports reflect the payout status

Challenges & Problem-Solving

Two representative problems from this module — how they were found and resolved, not just what the final test covered.

Ambiguous outcome after a bank-submission timeout
Challenge

A network timeout during bank submission left it ambiguous whether the transfer had actually succeeded on the bank's side before the timeout occurred.

Approach

Designed retry-idempotency tests that simulate a timeout occurring after the bank has already processed the transfer, not just before.

Outcome

Confirmed the Retry Service correctly checked live bank-side status before resubmitting, preventing a duplicate payout under this specific failure mode.

Beneficiary approval permission revoked mid-flow
Challenge

Beneficiary approval permissions were only being tested for the happy-path approver role, missing the case where an approver's permission was revoked mid-approval.

Approach

Treated permission-state changes during an in-progress approval as a first-class test scenario rather than an edge case.

Outcome

Surfaced a defect class around beneficiary permission edge cases that became a standing regression theme for every release.
